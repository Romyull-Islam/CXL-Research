\title{How KV Dies Decides How Flash Wears:\\
Endurance and Provisioning of Hybrid CXL Memory for LLM KV Caches}

\author{Romyull Islam}
\affiliation{\institution{(affiliation to be added)}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Hybrid CXL memory devices put a DRAM cache in front of terabytes of NAND flash and are proposed as
cheap capacity for the key-value (KV) cache of LLM serving. Prior designs evaluate hit rate and
latency, and flash-wear studies target on-package high-bandwidth flash; to our knowledge none asks how long a
hybrid CXL device lasts as a KV cache, or what to provision so that it lasts. We answer with six
production traces from two providers (chat, API, coding, reasoning and agent traffic), a model of
a hybrid device scaled consistently to the prefill throughput of one 8-GPU node, and a flash
translation layer validated against closed forms and the MQSim simulator. Three findings stand out.
First, how KV dies decides how flash wears: an LRU prefix cache evicts in near write order and keeps
write amplification near 1 (median 1.00, at most 1.94 at 7\% over-provisioning), while KV freed per request
interleaves lifetimes and reaches 2.1 with TRIM and 5.2 without it, the case on CXL.mem, which has
no TRIM. Second, the write rate itself is the wall: without admission control a node at full prefill
duty writes 280 to 1,290\,TB of BF16 KV to flash per day; with it, TLC lasts five years only for API-like traffic at partial
prefill duty or for an MLA model with 4-bit KV on the agent, conversation and reasoning traces, and interactive traffic needs pseudo-SLC or SLC.
Third, the cheap levers are admission control and KV precision (FP8 and INT4 halve and quarter the
writes, about one flash class each), not device DRAM, which changes NAND writes little. We turn
these results into provisioning rules across flash type, KV precision, admission and prefill duty,
and show that a shared pool beats load-balanced, cache-aware routing by only 1 to 18\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Long contexts, multi-turn chat and agents make LLM serving reuse large prefixes, and serving
systems keep the KV cache of those prefixes beyond GPU memory to avoid recomputing
them~\cite{mooncake,lmcache,kvwild}. CXL lets a server attach terabytes of memory next to its GPUs.
Hybrid CXL devices such as Samsung's CMM-H put a DRAM cache of tens of gigabytes in front of
terabytes of NAND flash~\cite{cmmh}, and several recent systems build KV caches on them:
HyMCache~\cite{hymcache}, ITME~\cite{itme}, LM-CXD~\cite{lmcxd} and TempoKV~\cite{tempokv}. They
report hit rates, time to first token and throughput. None measures wear: HyMCache's text never
mentions endurance, ITME discusses it only qualitatively, and LM-CXD and TempoKV do not discuss it.

Flash wears out after a few thousand program/erase cycles for TLC. A KV cache writes every block it
admits, and an LLM node computes new KV at gigabytes per second. Whether a hybrid device can absorb
that stream for its rated lifetime is the first question a device maker or an operator has to
answer. Recent work on on-package high-bandwidth flash (HBF) answers parts of it: on the
Qwen-Bailian traces a flash KV tier writes 1.1 to 4.9 times more than it reads, 48 to 140\,TB per
day per system, against a 21.7\,TB/day TLC budget~\cite{hbfsucks}; KV pages with different
lifetimes mixed in one erase block drive garbage-collection write amplification up to about
30~\cite{hbflex}; lifetime-aware placement and admission extend HBF life~\cite{lachesis,huawei_hbf,berkeley_hbf};
and TRIM has been argued for CXL memory in general~\cite{trimcxl}. None of this models a
DRAM-over-NAND CXL device, contrasts the ways KV dies, sweeps flash type and KV precision, or
scales production traces to a node.

This paper answers the question for hybrid CXL devices and turns the answer into provisioning
rules. To our knowledge it is the first endurance study of hybrid DRAM-over-NAND CXL devices as KV
caches. We make four contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item A trace-driven model of a hybrid device (DRAM cache over NAND, three admission policies) with
consistent per-node scaling: one node is modeled as $S$ interleaved copies of a trace, with $S$
chosen so that the node's prefill compute is saturated, and the device divided accordingly
(Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item The finding that how KV dies decides write amplification, measured with a page-mapped FTL
validated against Desnoyers' closed forms~\cite{desnoyers} and MQSim~\cite{mqsim}: an LRU KV cache
stays near 1, KV freed per request reaches 2.1 with TRIM and 5.2 without it, and CXL.mem's lack of
TRIM therefore costs 2.5 times for request-scoped KV (Section~\ref{sec:waf}).
\item Per-node wear across six traces, three models (GQA 70B, MLA DeepSeek-V3, GQA 7B), three KV
precisions and two prefill duties, and the cheapest flash class (TLC at 1 or 3 drive writes per
day, pseudo-SLC, SLC) that lasts five years in each case (Section~\ref{sec:prov}).
\item Two supporting analyses: when loading KV from the device beats recomputing it
(Section~\ref{sec:load}) and how much a shared pool gains over per-host caches at Mooncake's
scale (Section~\ref{sec:pool}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:bg}
\textbf{Hybrid CXL memory.} A CXL Type-3 device exposes memory to the host over CXL.mem. A hybrid
device backs that memory with NAND and keeps a DRAM cache: the CMM-H prototype has a 16\,GB
8-way cache with 4\,KB blocks over a 1\,TB TLC SSD and reaches 4.5\,GB/s~\cite{cmmh}; a newer
version has a 48\,GB cache, about 27\,GB/s when the working set fits and about 5\,GB/s when it
does not. ITME's miss path reaches 9\,GB/s~\cite{itme}. The host sees memory, not a block device:
CXL.mem has no TRIM, so the device learns that data is dead only when the host overwrites it.

\textbf{Flash endurance.} Enterprise drives are rated in drive writes per day (DWPD) over five
years. Read-intensive TLC drives are rated 1 DWPD and mixed-use TLC drives 3 DWPD (Samsung PM9A3,
Micron 7450 PRO and MAX, Kioxia CM7-R and CM7-V). TLC run as SLC gives a third of the capacity at
35 random-write DWPD (Micron XTR), and native SLC drives reach 50 DWPD (Solidigm D7-P5810). Ratings
assume random 4\,KB writes; large sequential writes are rated 1.7 to 4.4 times higher for the same
TLC drive. Write amplification (WAF), the flash pages programmed per page written by the host,
multiplies the wear; with greedy garbage collection under uniform random writes it is about 7 at
7\% over-provisioning and 2.4 at 28\%~\cite{desnoyers,vanhoudt}.

\textbf{KV caches.} Serving engines split a prompt into blocks of 16 to 512 tokens identified by a
hash of the prefix. A request reuses the longest cached prefix of its blocks and recomputes the
rest~\cite{vllm,mooncake}. A block is worth keeping only if a later request reuses it.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Traces.} Table~\ref{tab:traces} lists the six traces. Mooncake's conversation and
tool-agent traces cover one hour of Kimi traffic with 512-token blocks~\cite{mooncake}; the four
Qwen-Bailian traces cover two hours each of chat, API, coding and reasoning traffic with 16-token
blocks~\cite{bailian}. The share of blocks that are ever reused spans 6\% (API) to 47\% (chat).

\textbf{Device model.} The device has an LRU DRAM cache (48\,GB) over an LRU NAND tier (2\,TB,
or 667\,GB for the same NAND in pseudo-SLC mode). Requests read their blocks as a prefix: blocks
found in DRAM or NAND are hits until the first miss, after which blocks are recomputed and written
to DRAM. A block read from NAND is promoted to DRAM and stays in NAND. A block evicted from DRAM
is written to NAND if the admission policy accepts it and NAND does not hold it. We compare three
policies: \emph{all}; \emph{second hit}, which admits blocks referenced at least twice so far, as
in second-hit caching of flash~\cite{flashield}; and \emph{oracle}, which admits a block only if
it will be referenced again. The oracle writes the fewest blocks that still keep every hit the
device can serve; second hit writes 0.73 to 1.13 times as much but loses 2 to 41\% of the
oracle's hits (median 15\%), because the second reference often arrives after the block has left
the DRAM cache.

\textbf{Per-node scaling.} A trace is one stream of users; a node serving $S$ times its traffic
interleaves $S$ independent streams, which stretches reuse distances about $S$-fold. We model the
node by dividing the device's DRAM and NAND by $S$ and find $S$ as a fixed point:
\begin{equation}
G \cdot F \cdot u \cdot d \,/\, (2P) = S \cdot m(C/S),
\end{equation}
where the left side is the node's prefill throughput in tokens per second ($G=8$ H100 GPUs of
$F=989$ dense TFLOP/s at utilization $u=0.5$, prefill duty $d$, $P$ active parameters) and $m(C/S)$
is the trace's miss rate in tokens per second on a device of capacity $C/S$. The NAND write rate
is $S$ times the trace's. $S$ is 0.3 to 7 for Llama-3.1-70B, 0.5 to 14 for DeepSeek-V3 and 2.4
to 65 for Qwen2.5-7B. Prefill duty is 100\% for a dedicated prefill node and 30\% for a node that
also decodes.

\textbf{Models and precision.} Llama-3.1-70B (GQA, 320\,KB of BF16 KV per token),
DeepSeek-V3~\cite{deepseekv3} (MLA, 69\,KB) and Qwen2.5-7B (GQA, 56\,KB). FP8 and INT4 KV halve and
quarter the bytes per token, which also enlarges the device's capacity in blocks.

\textbf{Flash translation layer.} The NAND write stream (one entry per block written, naming the
slot it overwrites) drives a page-mapped FTL with greedy garbage collection that, like MQSim and most
SSD firmware, writes relocated data to its own block, with 64\,MB erase blocks
and 7\% or 28\% over-provisioning. Table~\ref{tab:ftl} validates the FTL against the closed forms
for uniform random writes~\cite{desnoyers}, and Table~\ref{tab:mqsim} against MQSim~\cite{mqsim},
a validated SSD simulator, on three of our KV write streams; with MQSim's reserve of 10 free blocks
the two agree within 4.5\% on the KV streams and within 11\% on uniform random writes. When a trace is too short for the NAND to be
overwritten several times, we append copies of the trace with new block identifiers (new users)
for the write-amplification measurement only; for 25\% of the 2\,TB cases and 10\% of the 667\,GB cases even that is not enough and
we take write amplification as 1.

\textbf{Budgets.} Five-year random-write ratings on a 2\,TB device: TLC-1, 2\,TB/day; TLC-3,
6\,TB/day; pseudo-SLC, 667\,GB at 35 DWPD, 23\,TB/day; SLC, 2\,TB at 50 DWPD, 100\,TB/day. A case
fits a class if its NAND writes, including write amplification at 7\% over-provisioning for TLC-1
and 28\% otherwise, stay within the budget.

\section{NAND write rates per node}
\label{sec:wear}
Figure~\ref{fig:wear} shows the NAND writes per node at full prefill duty with BF16 KV. Admitting
everything writes 281 to 319\,TB/day for DeepSeek-V3, 714 to 793 for Llama-3.1-70B and 1,174 to
1,291 for Qwen2.5-7B, two orders of magnitude above TLC and above any SLC budget, and 1.3 to 5.3
times more than the device ever reads back. Second-hit and oracle admission cut this by 2 to 20
times, most for the API trace, where only 6\% of blocks are reused. Per node, small models write
the most because their nodes prefill the most tokens (1.7 to 2.2 times Llama-3.1-70B), and MLA
writes 2.5 to 2.8 times less than Llama-3.1-70B because its KV is 4.7 times smaller.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_wear.pdf}
\caption{NAND writes per node at full prefill duty, BF16 KV, 28\% over-provisioning, with the
five-year budgets of a 2\,TB device.}
\label{fig:wear}

Two common shortcuts understate wear. Computing hit rates on a full device and multiplying the
write rate by the node's throughput, without dividing the device by $S$, understates the writes of
small models by up to 1.8 times. Taking write amplification as 1, as prior flash KV
studies do~\cite{hbfsucks}, understates them by up to 55\% on the 2\,TB device and 94\% on the
pseudo-SLC device at 7\% over-provisioning even for an LRU cache, and by 5.2 times for
request-scoped KV without TRIM (Section~\ref{sec:waf}).

\section{Write amplification depends on how KV dies}
\label{sec:waf}
Figure~\ref{fig:waf} (left) shows the measured write amplification over all prefix-cache
configurations. The median is 1.00. At 28\% over-provisioning it never exceeds 1.07 on the
2\,TB device and 1.18 on the smaller pseudo-SLC device; at 7\% oracle and second-hit admission
reach 1.55 and 1.94: the hits they keep refresh blocks in the NAND tier's
LRU order, so blocks no longer die in the order they were written.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_waf.pdf}
\caption{Left: distribution of write amplification over the sweep. Right: write amplification by
how KV dies, on the Bailian thinking trace with 64 KV blocks per erase block.}
\label{fig:waf}
\end{figure}

The right panel isolates the cause. An LRU cache evicts blocks roughly in write order, so whole
erase blocks die together and garbage collection copies almost nothing: write amplification 1.00
to 1.02. This matches the 1.02 that HiFC measured on a real drive~\cite{hifc} and the 1.15 that
SPLASH assumes~\cite{splash}. If instead KV is kept per request and freed when the request ends,
as when decode KV of reasoning requests is offloaded, concurrent requests interleave their blocks in
every erase block and end at different times: write amplification is 2.1 at 7\% over-provisioning
with TRIM. CXL.mem has no TRIM, so freed blocks stay valid in the FTL until the host reuses their
addresses, and write amplification rises to 5.2, 2.5 times the TRIM case. HBFlex reports about 30
for fine-grained LLM states mixed in coarse HBF blocks~\cite{hbflex}; the mechanism is the same, and
the gap reflects a different device, mapping granularity and spare space, so the two numbers bracket
the effect rather than contradict each other. Lee et al.\ argue for TRIM in CXL memory in general
and measure a 19.7\% write-amplification cut for virtual-machine workloads~\cite{trimcxl}; for
request-scoped KV the cut is 60\%, where whole requests die at once and
the serving engine knows when. A hybrid CXL device that stores request-scoped KV therefore needs either generous
over-provisioning or a way for the host to declare data dead, and a serving engine that frees KV
in LRU order avoids the problem.

\section{Provisioning}
\label{sec:prov}
Table~\ref{tab:prov} gives the cheapest flash class that lasts five years with second-hit
admission. Four rules follow.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Admission control is mandatory.} Admit-all never fits TLC or pseudo-SLC, and at full
prefill duty it fits nothing in 48 of 54 cases.
\item \emph{Workload decides the flash class.} TLC fits API-like traffic at partial prefill duty
(6 of 9 cases), but reasoning traffic only in 1 of 9 and interactive traffic (chat, coding,
conversation and agent) only in 2 of 36, all DeepSeek-V3 with INT4 KV. Interactive
traffic needs pseudo-SLC or SLC, and at full prefill duty 16 of its 36 cases exceed even
100\,TB/day.
\item \emph{KV precision is worth about one flash class.} FP8 halves and INT4 quarters the NAND
writes (0.49 and 0.24 times BF16 on average).
\item \emph{Model architecture matters as much as traffic.} MLA writes 2.5 to 2.8 times less than
a GQA 70B model per node, and small GQA models write the most.
\end{enumerate}
Using the higher sequential ratings for TLC moves a few cases from pseudo-SLC to TLC but changes
none of the rules. Pseudo-SLC's smaller capacity (667\,GB) costs little: the median hit share drops
by 1.4 points and the write rate is unchanged in the median. A second wall is bandwidth: 8\% of the
cases need more than the 4.5\,GB/s that a measured CMM-H sustains on its NAND path, all of them
with admit-all or Qwen2.5-7B.

\textbf{Device DRAM size.} Growing the device's DRAM cache from 16 to 256\,GB (Bailian API, chat
and thinking traces, Llama-3.1-70B and DeepSeek-V3, BF16, full duty) changes the NAND writes by
$-32$ to $+6\%$, and by under 5\% in most cases, while the share of hits served from DRAM rises
from 0.24--0.49 to 0.35--0.90 (results/dram\_sensitivity.csv). A larger DRAM cache improves hit
latency but not lifetime: in a node serving many users, a block's reuse arrives long after it has
left any affordable DRAM cache.

\section{When does loading beat recomputing?}
\label{sec:load}
A hit served from the device pays only if loading the KV is faster than recomputing it. Recomputing
$n$ tokens on the node takes $t(n) = (2Pn + Lwn^2)/(G F u)$, with $w$ the attention width; loading
takes $n b / B$ for $b$ bytes of KV per token over a path of $B$ bytes per second. The break-even
bandwidth $B^* = nb/t(n)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:load}) falls with context length because attention makes
recompute superlinear; the trade-off itself is formalized in~\cite{restore_recompute,pykvcache}.
On 8 H100 GPUs, hits served from the device's DRAM cache (about 27\,GB/s) always pay. Hits from
NAND (about 4.5\,GB/s) pay for DeepSeek-V3 at every precision, for Llama-3.1-70B only with FP8
(borderline at 2K tokens) or INT4 KV, and for Qwen2.5-7B only with INT4. On B200 GPUs the break-even rises about 2.3 times and
NAND hits stop paying for most BF16 cases. Lower KV precision thus helps twice: it cuts writes and
makes NAND hits worth serving.

\section{Shared pool or per-host caches?}
\label{sec:pool}
A CXL pool shared by several prefill hosts holds each block once and serves every host; per-host
caches duplicate popular blocks. Figure~\ref{fig:pool} compares a pool with per-host caches under
random and cache-aware routing; cache-aware routing sends a request to the host with the longest
cached prefix unless that host's load exceeds the mean by more than 25\%. Over 4 to 16 hosts and
total capacities of 2 to 50\% of the unique blocks, the pool hits 1.05 to 4.5 times as often as
random routing and writes 1.03 to 1.9 times fewer blocks, but beats cache-aware routing by only
1 to 18\% in hits and 1 to 9\% in writes. Mooncake reports up to 136\% more hits for its global
pool with a cache-aware scheduler on 10 nodes of 3M tokens each~\cite{mooncake}. Reproducing that
setup, the pool gains 3 to 10\% when the scheduler allows 5 to 100\% imbalance, 15 to 43\% when it
balances exactly and up to 165\% when it ignores load (Figure~\ref{fig:pool}, right). The value of
a pool therefore depends mostly on how strictly the scheduler balances load, which also bounds what
a pooled CXL tier saves in wear.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\textbf{Scaling.} Modeling a node as $S$ interleaved copies of a trace assumes independent users;
real routing can concentrate a conversation on one node, which shortens reuse distances and favors
the cache. The traces cover one to two hours, so daily cycles are not represented.

\textbf{Write amplification.} Our FTL matches closed forms but not a specific drive; a quarter of
the cases are too short to reach steady state and use write amplification 1. Devices with large
superblocks or internal DRAM write coalescing will differ.

\textbf{Retention.} KV needs minutes to hours of retention, not the months that enterprise ratings
assume; relaxing retention to days raises flash endurance by up to 50 times in published
characterizations~\cite{warm}, and SPLASH exploits a 30-minute retention for HBF~\cite{splash}. A
hybrid CXL device that knew its contents were KV could trade retention for endurance, which would
move most interactive cases from SLC to TLC. We leave this to future work.

\textbf{Related work.} Endurance of flash KV tiers has been studied for HBF: HBF Sucks?
(\cite{hbfsucks}), HBFlex (lifetime-aware placement, \cite{hbflex}), Lachesis~\cite{lachesis},
admission for HBF~\cite{huawei_hbf,berkeley_hbf}, SPLASH~\cite{splash} and HiFC with pseudo-SLC on
a consumer SSD~\cite{hifc}. Lee et al.\ argue for TRIM in CXL memory and evaluate it on
virtual-machine workloads~\cite{trimcxl}. Hybrid CXL KV systems evaluate performance without
wear~\cite{hymcache,itme,lmcxd,tempokv}, and device studies characterize CMM-H performance~\cite{cmmh,cylon}.
KVCache-in-the-Wild~\cite{kvwild}, Mooncake~\cite{mooncake} and TokenLake~\cite{tokenlake}
characterize reuse and pooling. We add the hybrid device model, consistent per-node scaling,
measured write amplification including the no-TRIM case, and provisioning across flash type, KV
precision, admission and prefill duty, and we show that the way the serving engine frees KV, not
the device, decides write amplification.

\textbf{Implications.} For serving engines: keep KV on a hybrid device as an LRU prefix cache, or
free request-scoped KV in an order the device can follow, and admit only reused blocks. For device
designers: a way for the host to declare data dead, such as the TRIM for CXL memory proposed by Lee
et al.~\cite{trimcxl}, is worth 2.5 times in write amplification for request-scoped KV, and
relaxed retention could move interactive traffic to cheaper flash. For operators: the provisioning
rules of Section~\ref{sec:prov} pick the flash class from the traffic type, KV precision and
prefill duty.

\section{Conclusion}
How KV dies decides how flash wears. An LRU KV cache on a hybrid CXL device keeps write
amplification near 1, while request-scoped KV on CXL.mem, which has no TRIM, raises it to 5.2.
Even near 1, the write rate itself is the wall: a hybrid device lasts five years on TLC flash only
when it caches API-like traffic with admission control at partial prefill duty, or 4-bit MLA KV
for agent, conversation or reasoning traffic. Interactive traffic needs pseudo-SLC or SLC, or a device that trades retention for endurance.
Admission control and lower KV precision are the cheapest levers.
